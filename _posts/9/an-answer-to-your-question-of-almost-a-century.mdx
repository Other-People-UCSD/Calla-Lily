<p style="text-align: center;">
An Answer to your Question of Almost a Century<br />
by Thy Ngo<br />
<i>Written in honor of my grandfather</i>
</p>


Your blurred face stares at me across the weathered table as the sun<br />
bears its heat through the blinds. From your gentle voice I hear the words:<br />
“Thầy, I want to know your story before you are gone.”<br />
<br />
You are asking me to give you my history<br />
and fill your vessel with my wisdom while<br />
I have survived 94 years. You, my youngest son,<br />
the playful one, came with my 10 other children to this southern land of marshes to<br />
celebrate 100 years of life I have not yet reached.<br />
You’re afraid you’ll see my grave before<br />
the digits strike three.<br />
Here I am fulfilling your plea, recounting my life’s story. A narrative incomplete in my decaying <br />
memory, yet more vivid than my fading knowledge <br />
of birthdays and your children’s names.<br />
<br />
I was only four years younger than you:<br />
48 when I made the journey.<br />
I hauled you and your siblings onto the rickety boat<br />
that would take us to safety.<br />
My heart grieves each day with the guilt<br />
of not having left sooner—for your mother,<br />
shot dead at the point of a gun,<br />
could’ve been spared to care for you, son.<br />
<br />
You and my little Lien had not even lived a decade. But my steely<br />
exterior hardened by pride refused to let go <br />
of the place I call home, fervently wanting my children to grow<br />
on the rice-fertile plains in the land I was born, being comforted<br />
by the waters that rocked my soul to sleep.<br />
I desired for your eyes to wander at the docking of boats <br />
come dawn, the sea calling with the whispers of a hundred cá lung linh. <br />
But my love was lost, and I was left to be alone—<br />
to be the nurturing hand<br />
to nourish, to feed, to care <br />
as a grieving man.<br />
<br />
The journey to the frigid place across the ocean tumbled with the tides of my grief. <br />
I couldn’t lose sight of you—my children—or my spirit would be lost. So I <br />
summoned courage from God and God’s Mother through our Fathers and Hail Marys, <br />
recited under my breath as I nudged your sleepy body to repeat.<br />
I urged you to hold on tight to your sisters and brothers, <br />
watching as the frantic, innocent eyes of my youngest son<br />
searched for a sign of the sun. We pleaded to Heaven as our boat was tugged by God’s ocean and<br />
I held your warm chest to my aching heart.<br />
<br />
We crammed into air-tight bedrooms in the snow-cold <br />
land of Chicago where I watched you <br />
play with white boys and chase<br />
wild rabbits with blunted knives. When my shift at the school ended, <br />
I brought home day-old donuts for dinner,<br />
staring with the weight of a rock in my stomach as<br />
my children ate stale cakes like they were from Heaven’s bakery. <br />
“Don’t worry, kids,” I jested, “I washed my hands after cleaning the toilets.”<br />
I used your laughter to fill the void of losing my love<br />
as I soothed your empty stomachs with almost-expired sweets <br />
passed along to me in pity.<br />
<br />
I watched in silence as life stole your mother<br />
from you and then your white-boy friend when we migrated South<br />
to Louisiana: a name I still can’t pronounce.<br />
I worked night shifts as you wrapped <br />
your tongue around a dialogue and<br />
befriended Black kids on the block.<br />
The landscape here is sun-charred and humid,<br />
reminding me of my rice-fertile home across the sea but <br />
without the comfort of the ocean <br />
luring me from sleep come morning.<br />
<br />
Only 15 years passed when you left your Black friends behind.<br />
It was you who made the choice, this time—<br />
not the soldiers in the marshes<br />
or your weary father with softening bones and a hardened facial expression.<br />
<br />
You moved with your brothers to a land of sunshine where you <br />
settled close to the ocean and had two children.<br />
You never returned to the country where I nourished your fragile life<br />
52 years ago,<br />
my lungs breathing in the salt of the ocean<br />
that bound me to decades-long grief.<br />
<br />
I don’t remember the name<br />
of the little girl you now tell to kiss me on the cheek and say, “Happy Birthday, ông nội—<br />
thank you for telling me your story.”<br />
But I am glad you came to see me, asking<br />
to record my story in youthful memory<br />
before my digits strike three. <br />
